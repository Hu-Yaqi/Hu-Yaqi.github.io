A choice, a brain response, or a written account each shows part of what a person is thinking, and each is limited in its own way. My work asks how to recover what is real from such sources: knowing what a source shows, knowing how it is limited, and reasoning past that limit. I combine computational models of behavior and neural responses with analyses of how people describe their experience, and I test each method in cases where the truth is known.

Rethinking Behavioral Drift: Non-Parametric Measures of Choice Inconsistency

2026

Hu, Y. Manuscript in preparation for journal submission.

Do decision rules drift within a single experiment? Fitting a utility model run by run can report a change that comes only from which gambles were offered. I built two measures instead, decision-boundary violations and matched flip scores, on a single assumption: a person who accepts a gamble should also accept one that is better on both gain and loss.

Validated on simulated agents with known behavior, the measures show that drift is real but brief: an early warm-up in which people more often accept a gamble while rejecting a better one, mostly gone within about 25 trials. The pattern appears in the NARPS mixed-gambles data, in five more laboratory datasets, and in a smartphone study of 47,067 participants. In exploratory trial-level fMRI analyses, choices that contradicted a person’s own rule came with a weaker dorsal putamen response.

How Do LLMs Read Our Minds? Magic, Human Accounts, and Ground Truth

2026–present

M.A. project, Media Studies and Production, Temple University. Website and study design in progress.

A magic trick is a black box whose inside is known. I am building an interactive narrative website that appears to read minds: after a series of games, it predicts which of three options each participant will pick. Everyone experiences the same effect and misses the same setup, so their accounts share a blind spot that averaging cannot remove.

Participants will describe the session and explain how they think the site knew. Large language models will receive only these texts, and their answers will be checked against the session logs and the known method. The study asks whether a model can reason past a shared blind spot, whether it separates what people saw from what they inferred, and whether people’s explanations help it or anchor it to their mistakes. The project is also a media artwork on living with opaque technologies.

What Do We Mean by Dating? Chinese Social Media and the Changing Language of Intimacy

2025

Course research project, Communication Research Methods, Temple University.

A corpus of 10,400 Chinese social-media posts in which people describe their dating lives and credit their choices to themselves, their culture, or their circumstances. I adapted an open-source scraping framework, built the cleaning and preprocessing routines, ran exploratory topic clustering and keyword extraction, and developed a preliminary thematic codebook. Further analysis is in progress.
